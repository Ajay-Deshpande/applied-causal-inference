The problem

Why correlation keeps getting people fired

1
Correlation ≠ Causation

Ice cream sales and drowning rates are correlated. Neither causes the other — summer does both. The same logic applies to most business metrics.

2
Selection Bias

Users who click on premium features are not comparable to those who don't. The click caused the difference, or did a pre-existing difference cause the click?

3
The Fundamental Problem

You can only observe each unit in one state — treated or untreated. The counterfactual ("what would have happened otherwise?") is always missing. Every causal method is a different strategy to reconstruct it.

Why A/B Testing is the Gold Standard — and What Breaks When You Can't Use It

Before learning four causal methods, you need to understand the one method that needs no assumptions. Then you need to see it fail. That's what this phase is about.

Start with a question, not a method

Your team redesigned a product page. Someone in a Monday meeting says "let's check if the new page performs better." You pull a report showing users who saw the new page converted at 13%, versus 10% for the old page. A three percentage point lift. Ship it?

Not yet. That 3pp difference has two possible explanations: the new design caused more purchases, or the users who happened to see the new design were already more likely to buy. You cannot tell which is true from that number alone. This is the fundamental problem of causal inference — and it comes up in every business decision involving data.

"Randomization doesn't just control for confounders you can think of. It controls for confounders you haven't thought of yet."

What makes an A/B test causal

An A/B test is a Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT). You randomly assign each user to either the old page (control) or the new page (treatment). The word "randomly" is doing enormous work here.

When assignment is truly random, every characteristic of a user — their age, their purchase history, their device, their income, their mood — is equally distributed across both groups in expectation. The treated and control groups are statistically identical before the experiment starts. So any difference in outcomes afterwards can only come from one place: the thing you changed.

This is why the naive mean difference is the causal effect in a proper A/B test:

ATE = E[Y | D=1] − E[Y | D=0] = Ȳ_treated − Ȳ_control

No adjustment. No modeling. No assumptions beyond "the randomization actually worked." This simplicity is the gold standard everyone else is trying to approximate.

The estimand: ATE

We're estimating the Average Treatment Effect (ATE) — the average causal effect across the entire user population, both those who would be treated and those who wouldn't. Formally:

ATE = E[Y(1) − Y(0)]

Where Y(1) is a user's outcome if they see the new page, and Y(0) is their outcome if they see the old page. The problem: you never observe both for the same user. Randomization lets you reconstruct this average across groups.

Building the dataset

We simulate 10,000 users with a known true effect of exactly 3 percentage points. Crucially, we also include covariates — age, device type, prior visits — that will become confounders in Phase 3 (PSM). This is intentional: one dataset, used across multiple phases, each time exploiting different structure.

TRUE_EFFECT = 0.03  # 3pp lift — the answer we want to recover

# Random assignment — the core of an A/B test
treatment = np.random.binomial(1, 0.5, N)

# Outcome probability — treatment adds 3pp on top of covariates
log_odds = (
    -2.5
    + TRUE_EFFECT * 4 * treatment
    + 0.02 * (age - 35)
    + 0.05 * prior_visits
)
purchased = np.random.binomial(1, sigmoid(log_odds), N)

The DGP (data generating process) is the true model that created your data. In real life you never know it. Here you do — which is what makes simulation so powerful for learning. Your estimator should recover 0.03.

Three estimators, same answer

We estimate ATE three different ways — not because we need to, but to build intuition for what an estimator actually is. In a proper RCT, all three converge on the same number.

Key insightAdding covariates to the OLS regression makes the standard error smaller (more precise) but barely changes the point estimate. That's because covariates are uncorrelated with treatment under randomization — they explain residual variance in Y but not the treatment effect. In Phase 3, adding covariates will change the estimate dramatically. That difference is worth internalizing now.

Confidence intervals: analytical vs bootstrap

A point estimate without an interval is incomplete. We compute CIs two ways: analytically (from OLS standard errors, assumes normality) and via bootstrap (model-free, resamples data 2,000 times). Both give similar results because our sample is large. The bootstrap becomes essential in smaller samples or non-standard estimands.

The most important cell: breaking the experiment

We deliberately remove randomization and re-run the same analysis. Younger, more engaged users now self-select into the treatment group. The naive estimate inflates to roughly 6–8 percentage points — more than double the true 3pp effect. The SMD (Standardized Mean Difference) on age and prior visits jumps from near zero to 0.3+.

This is not a hypothetical. This is exactly what the LaLonde dataset in Phase 2 looks like. Workers who chose to enroll in job training were already different from those who didn't. The naive estimate is biased by exactly this mechanism.

"Without randomization, the naive estimate was 2× the true effect. That's not noise — that's selection bias, and it will mislead every decision made from it."

Where the gold standard breaks down

Randomization eliminates confounders — but only if the experiment is run correctly. In practice, A/B tests fail more often from execution mistakes than from bad statistical design. These are the four ways a well-intentioned experiment produces a wrong answer.

1. Peeking — stopping the experiment early

You launch a test, check the dashboard two days later, see p=0.03, and stop. The problem: p-values are designed to be interpreted at a single pre-specified sample size. Checking repeatedly — at day 1, day 2, day 3 — gives you multiple chances to cross the significance threshold by random chance. With 20 peeks, your actual false positive rate climbs from 5% to roughly 40%. You ship a change that did nothing, or worse, harmed users, with the full confidence of a "statistically significant" result.

The fix: commit to a sample size before the experiment starts and don't stop early. If your team genuinely needs continuous monitoring, use sequential testing methods — always-valid p-values or Bayesian frameworks — which are designed for this use case.

2. Insufficient power — not knowing your sample size

An underpowered experiment produces false negatives: your test finds "no effect" and you don't ship a change that would have genuinely helped users. Power analysis answers a single question — how many users do I need? — and requires four inputs you should know before touching any data: baseline conversion rate, minimum detectable effect (the smallest lift you'd actually care about), significance level (α, usually 0.05), and desired power (1−β, usually 0.80).

These four inputs produce a required sample size per group. The practical failure: teams skip this step, run a test for one week because that feels right, and conclude "no effect" from a sample that was never large enough to detect one. An underpowered non-result is not a result.

MDE is a business decision, not a statistical oneThe minimum detectable effect should be set based on what lift is actually worth shipping. A 0.01pp improvement may not justify engineering time; a 0.5pp improvement might. Set it before the experiment — not after you see the data.

3. P-hacking and multiple comparisons

You designed the experiment to measure purchase rate. But your dashboard shows 15 metrics. Three are significant. You report those. This is p-hacking — not necessarily malicious, but statistically identical to running 15 experiments and showing the best result. At α=0.05, you expect one false positive for every 20 tests by pure chance. With 15 metrics, the probability of at least one spurious significant result is around 54%.

The fix: pre-register your primary metric before the experiment starts. That's the one that drives the shipping decision. Secondary metrics are exploratory — they generate hypotheses for future tests, not conclusions from this one. When you do need to test multiple metrics formally, apply Bonferroni correction (divide α by the number of tests) or Benjamini-Hochberg (controls false discovery rate, less conservative). Your analysis plan should exist before you see the data.

4. Novelty effects — users responding to change, not improvement

Your new design shows a 3pp lift in week one. You ship it. Two months later, conversion is back to baseline. What happened: users clicked the new design because it was different — not because it was better. The novelty wore off. The A/B test captured a real short-term effect that wasn't the long-term effect you cared about.

Novelty effects are most common with UI changes — new layouts, colors, navigation patterns — and less common with structural changes like pricing or checkout flow, where users engage with the mechanism rather than noticing something different. The diagnostic: segment your treatment effect by how long a user has been in the experiment. If new entrants show a large effect and users who've been in the test for two or three weeks show near zero, you're likely measuring novelty. The fix is usually to run the experiment longer — but this requires having done the power analysis first, so you knew how long "long enough" was before you started.

The common threadAll four failures produce a number that looks like a causal estimate but isn't one. Peeking gives you false positives. Insufficient power gives you false negatives. P-hacking gives you cherry-picked results. Novelty gives you a transient effect mistaken for a permanent one. The gold standard is only gold if the execution matches the design.

What you should take from Phase 1

Randomization is the only method that requires no assumptions about confounders — because it eliminates them by design. Every other method in this series is an attempt to approximate randomization when it's unavailable. DiD uses time variation. PSM uses observed covariates. Synthetic Control uses donor units. RD uses a threshold. Each approach makes a different assumption, and each assumption can be tested — but none of them is as clean as what you built here.

The discomfort you felt in the "broken experiment" cell — that feeling of not knowing whether your estimate is real — is what motivates the next four phases.

Difference-in-Differences: When Time Becomes Your Control Group

The LaLonde job training dataset has a known true effect of $1,794. The naive comparison gives answers ranging from −$8,000 to +$2,000 depending on the approach. DiD uses the time dimension to get closer to the truth — under one testable assumption.

The dataset that broke observational methods

In 1986, Robert LaLonde published a paper evaluating the JTPA job training program. He had data from a genuine RCT — workers randomly assigned to training or not — and published the true treatment effect: $1,794 increase in annual earnings.

Then he replaced the randomized control group with workers from the PSID survey — real workers who hadn't been trained, but hadn't been randomly assigned to anything. He asked: what does the naive observational comparison give you? The answers swung from −$8,498 to +$2,249. The true effect was $1,794. This dataset became the benchmark every causal method is tested against.

"The PSID control group earns $13,000/year before training. The treated group earns $2,000/year. You're not comparing two groups — you're comparing two different economic realities."

Why the naive comparison fails

Workers who enrolled in training were younger, less educated, earned far less, and were economically disadvantaged before any training occurred. Comparing their 1978 earnings to the PSID workers confounds the training effect with an $11,000 pre-existing gap. Selection bias here isn't subtle — it's the entire signal.

The DiD formula

Instead of comparing earnings levels, compare earnings changes. If the control group grew by $X between 1975 and 1978 due to macroeconomic forces, the treated group should have grown by the same $X without training. Any additional growth is the training effect.

DiD = (Treated₁₉₇₈ − Treated₁₉₇₅) − (Control₁₉₇₈ − Control₁₉₇₅)

In regression form: earnings ~ post + treat + post:treat. The coefficient on post:treat — the interaction — is the DiD estimate. It captures the additional earnings change for treated workers in the post-period, above and beyond what control workers experienced.

The parallel trends assumption

DiD works under one assumption: in the absence of treatment, the treated group's earnings would have followed the same trend as the control group. Not the same level — the same slope. The dashed line in Figure 4 is that counterfactual. You never observe it. You can only make it plausible.

Testing it: the pre-trends test

We have earnings from 1974 and 1975, both pre-treatment. Under parallel trends, the two groups should have moved similarly between these years. We run DiD on 1974→1975 only — treating 1975 as the "post" period and ignoring 1978. The interaction coefficient should be near zero and statistically insignificant. A large, significant pre-trend coefficient means the groups were already diverging before training — the assumption is in trouble.

What breaking parallel trends looks like

The notebook adds $3,000 to the control group's 1978 earnings — simulating a macroeconomic boom that hit PSID workers harder than the disadvantaged treated group. The DiD estimate drops: treatment looks less effective than it was. The direction of bias depends entirely on how the assumption is violated. There is no way to know the direction from data alone — which is exactly why the pre-trends test exists.

Key resultDiD estimates land in the $1,200–$2,000 range across specifications — meaningfully closer to the $1,794 benchmark than the naive comparison's swing from negative to wildly positive. The pre-trends test result tells you how much confidence to place in the design.

What DiD leaves unsolved

DiD required panel data — the same workers observed before and after. It also required a control group that trends in parallel, which is genuinely hard to defend with the PSID comparison. What if you only have a single cross-sectional snapshot? What if there's no pre-period available? You need a different strategy — one that constructs a comparable control group directly from covariate information rather than from time variation.

SERIES · PART 4 Propensity Score Matching ~14 min read LaLonde Dataset · Python

Propensity Score Matching: When the Method Tells You It Can't Help

We ran PSM on two versions of the LaLonde dataset — same treated workers, different control groups. On the first, the method failed completely and told us so. On the second, it worked marginally with documented limitations. That two-path result is the real lesson.

The prerequisite nobody emphasizes: overlap

Before matching a single pair, the propensity score model's AUC tells you whether matching is even possible. AUC 0.6–0.85 means the groups overlap enough to match. AUC above 0.95 means near-perfect separation — the groups are from fundamentally different populations and no algorithm can bridge that gap.

"A propensity model with 0.98 AUC isn't telling you the model is good — it's telling you the groups are incomparable. PSM correctly detected its own inapplicability."

Path A: PSID controls — correctly flagged as inapplicable

The PSID control workers average 34 years old with $13,000/year pre-training earnings. The treated workers average 25 years old with $2,000/year. The logistic propensity model achieves AUC ~0.98 on this combined dataset. The overlap plot shows two completely separated spikes. We documented the failure and moved on — running the matching anyway would have produced a meaningless number.

Path B: CPS controls — borderline, documented

The CPS-3 trimmed control group (429 workers) is closer to the treated workers demographically. AUC is lower — borderline acceptable. The distributions share a meaningful common support region. Matching is feasible but some treated workers are dropped by the caliper. We document exactly how many and note that the ATT estimate applies only to the matchable subgroup.

On KNN memory at scalesklearn's NearestNeighbors indexes the control propensity scores — O(n_control) memory in 1D. Fine for LaLonde. At millions of controls or when matching on raw covariates in high dimensions, this blows up. Solutions: approximate nearest neighbors (FAISS, Annoy), stratification on PS deciles, or coarsened exact matching (CEM). This is one reason PS-based methods are preferred over direct covariate matching — keeping the problem in 1D prevents the dimensional blowup.
On the Rosenbaum boundsWe include them but scope them carefully. Rosenbaum bounds are most interpretable when matching achieved clean balance. Here, some residual imbalance remains. Reporting the bounds as primary robustness evidence would overclaim — they are supplementary context on a borderline dataset.

Estimand is ATT — directly comparable to the $1,794 benchmark. Result: ATT = $1,212 on the full 185 treated workers, narrower CI than PSM's 83-pair estimate.

Reweighting Instead of Discarding: Inverse Probability Weighting

PSM dropped 102 of 185 treated workers and produced an estimate too noisy to use. IPW keeps every observation — reweighting the control group to look like the treated group. Same propensity model, entirely different strategy, and the best observational ATT estimate in the series so far: $1,212 against a benchmark of $1,794.

What PSM left unsolved

In Phase 3, the caliper matching algorithm matched 83 of 185 treated workers. The other 102 were dropped. The matched sample was too small to detect a $1,794 effect — SE ~$990, CI spanning $4,000, p-value above 0.6. The failure was not hidden confounding. It was data loss from a lossy matching operation.

IPW keeps every observation. Instead of finding a close match and discarding workers without one, it reweights the control group. Controls who look like the treated workers get upweighted; controls who look nothing like them get downweighted. No one is dropped.

Why ATT and not ATE

IPW is often described as naturally estimating the ATE — historically, because the Horvitz-Thompson (1952) estimator came from survey sampling where population-level inference was the goal. But for this problem, ATT is the right estimand.

The LaLonde RCT benchmark of $1,794 is an ATT: the effect on workers who actually enrolled in job training. ATE would measure the hypothetical effect if training were extended to the full CPS population — workers who would never realistically enroll, with different earnings histories and labor market attachment. We have no ground truth to compare an ATE against. ATT is the headline; ATE appears at the end of the notebook as a secondary result with its own interpretation.

The ATT weight formula: treated units keep weight 1. Control units get weight e(X) / (1 − e(X)) — how "treated-like" they are based on their propensity score. The reweighted control group becomes a synthetic comparison population that mirrors the treated group's covariate distribution.

Why AIPW performed worse than raw IPW

The AIPW estimate of $476 is substantially worse than trimmed IPW's $1,212. This deserves a direct explanation.

AIPW is doubly robust: consistent if either the propensity model or the outcome regression model is correctly specified — not both. The outcome model here is OLS linear regression on re78. Earnings are highly right-skewed with a spike at zero — linear regression predicts poorly, especially for treated workers with zero prior earnings. When the outcome model is misspecified, the AIPW augmentation terms — designed to correct for propensity model errors — add noise instead. The result is a higher-variance estimate that moves further from the benchmark.

This is not a failure of the method. It is a demonstration of what "doubly robust" actually means: one model must be correct, not neither. Phase 7 (Double ML) will use flexible nonlinear outcome models that can handle skewed earnings — which is precisely why DML tends to outperform AIPW with linear nuisance models.

Key takeawayIPW used all 185 treated workers and produced ATT = $1,212 — the best point estimate of the series so far. The CI barely clips zero. The improvement over PSM ($123–$468, n=83) is directly traceable to the larger effective sample. Overlap is still the binding constraint: Full CPS fails completely, for the same reason PSM failed in Phase 3. IPW doesn't fix overlap; it just measures the damage differently — through ESS collapse rather than sample discard.
